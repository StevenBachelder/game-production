# Game Production — Literature

APA 7th edition. Current edition first, original year noted where a work has been reissued
or revised. Three parts: the reference list, a map of where each text sits in the course,
and further reading suggestions per week.

## Getting access

- **Oria** (oria.no) with Feide resolves nearly everything below to full text. Thirteen
  titles are MIT Press, so ask the library whether INN holds the MIT Press Direct collection.
- **JSTOR** links open in full through INN; off campus, sign in institutionally first.
- **Nasjonalbiblioteket** (nb.no) gives free in-browser reading of Norwegian books from
  Norwegian IP addresses — check there for *Kamerakuren*.
- **Internet Archive** (archive.org) lends scans one reader at a time, free with a login.
  The quickest legitimate route to Dewey, Ostrom, Huizinga, Caillois and Piaget.
- **OPEN ACCESS** and **FREE** entries need no login. There are twelve — enough to build required reading
  around, since every student can reach them on day one.

ISBNs are for ordering; confirm at point of purchase.

## Where the literature sits in the course

Core = the two or three that carry the week. Supporting = read if the team's project points
that way. Nothing here assumes more than one book per week.

### Week 1 · Samling 1 — Introduction
**Core:** Lantz, *The Beauty of Games* (pp. 1–164) — the best single answer to "what is a
game and why does it matter", and the right tone to open on.
**Supporting:** Sicart, *Play Matters*.

### Week 2 · Game design, game studies, game theory, system dynamics
**Core:** Salen & Zimmerman, *Rules of Play* — the field's shared vocabulary.
Samuelson (2016) for game theory proper, and it is free.
**Supporting:** Karabinus et al., *Historiographies of Game Studies*, for what game studies
has been and could be. Free.

### Week 3 · Narrative structure and narratology
**Core:** Murray, *Hamlet on the Holodeck*.
**Supporting:** Jenkins & Squire, "The art of contested spaces" — narrative as spatial
arrangement, which is exactly your "sequential and/or spatial" framing. Free.
Clark & Kelomees, *[re|dis]connection* — artists writing about their own interactive
storytelling work. Free, and a useful counterweight to Murray's theory.

### Week 4 · System thinking and system design
**Core:** Meadows, *Thinking in Systems* — read before the CLD workshop, not after.
**Supporting:** Salen & Zimmerman's systems chapters, revisited from week 2.

### Week 5 · System dynamics in society, the commons, win-states
**Core:** Hardin (1968) and Ostrom (1990), read as a pair. Hardin states the trap; Ostrom
shows the empirical exits from it. Assigning only Hardin produces a much duller brief.
**Supporting:** Bogost, *Persuasive Games* — how a rule system argues, which is what a
commons model does.

### Week 6 · Samling 2
Consolidation before green-lighting; no new reading.

### Week 7 · World building, production, pipelines
**Core:** Macklin & Sharp, *Games, Design and Play* — iterative process and the pitch.
**Supporting:** Fullerton, *Game Design Workshop*, on prototyping and the green-light.
Walz, *Toward a Ludic Architecture* — how play and games are architected in space. Free, and
the closest thing on the list to a world-building text.

### Week 8 · Rules, mechanics, affordances — the gears of the game
**Core:** Salen & Zimmerman on rules and mechanics; Swink, *Game Feel*, on what the gears
feel like in the hand.
**Supporting:** Costikyan, *Uncertainty in Games*; Wing, *Bounce* (free), whose account of
"bounce feel" in FIFA is a case study in Swink's sense of the term. Zagal, *Game Design
Snacks* (free) — short, concrete and grounded in released games, so it suits a team that
wants examples rather than theory.

### Week 9 · Artistic intentionality
**Core:** Nguyen, *Games: Agency as Art* — designers shape not only what players do but what
they want while playing. Sharp, *Works of Game*, for the games-as-art framing.
**Supporting:** Clark & Kelomees, *[re|dis]connection* (free) — twenty-odd artists stating
their intentions in their own words, which is the exercise week 9 asks of the teams.
Dewey, *Art as Experience*, and Greene, *Releasing the Imagination*, for the aesthetic
ground. Flanagan, *Critical Play*, and Sicart (2013) where the intention is
political or ethical. Isbister, *How Games Move Us*, if the intention is emotional.

### Week 10 · Game analysis, peer to peer
**Core:** Lankoski & Björk, *Game Research Methods*, analysis chapters. Free, so this can be
required of everyone.
**Supporting:** Sharp, *Works of Game*, as a worked example of critical analysis.

### Week 11 · Review of student works; continuation of 4, 8 and 9
No new reading. Send teams back to Meadows, Swink and Nguyen with their prototype in hand.

### Week 12 · Samling 3
Consolidation before testing; no new reading.

### Weeks 13–14 · Research methods, play-testing, exam
**Core:** Lankoski & Björk on playtesting and qualitative method (free); Fullerton's
playtesting chapters.
**Supporting:** Lankoski & Holopainen, *Game Design Research* (free) — research through
designing, which is what an MFA production actually is. Ke (2016) as a model of how a
systematic review is built, for anyone whose project makes a learning claim.

### Week 15 · Samling 4 — The phenomena of play and the structure of games
This is where the authors named in your course document finally have titles attached.
**Core:** Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, for affordances and the
development of perception. Sutton-Smith, *The Ambiguity of Play*, for play as driver rather
than product of culture. Huizinga, *Homo Ludens*, as the text they are both arguing with.
**Supporting:** De Koven, *A Playful Path* (free), for playfulness as a disposition rather
than an activity. Goffman, *Encounters* ("Fun in Games"), for the frame that holds play
together. Vygotsky and Piaget for play in development. Upton, *The Aesthetic of Play*, and
Sicart, *Play Matters*, for the contemporary reframing. Wing, *Bounce* (free), for play as a
material and bodily matter.

### Week 16 and Week 1 / 2027 · Peeling the onion
**Core:** Dewey, *Art as Experience* — reading the finished experience back against the
intention is close to Dewey's own argument about how an experience becomes *an* experience.
**Supporting:** Greene, *Releasing the Imagination*; Færø, *Kamerakuren*, on attention and
the moment, which fits the reflective register of these weeks.

### Not tied to a single week
Gee, McGonigal, Squire (2007), Squire & Jenkins (2003), Cassell & Jenkins, Kafai et al.,
Mahar, and Johnson & Salter are context rather than instruction — games and learning, games
and gender, moral panic, AI and making. Point students at them when a project turns that
way rather than assigning them by date.

### Affordances — the Gibson/Norman divergence (weeks 8 and 15)

All of these are now in the reference list above; this is the map through them.

For Gibson an affordance is a **relation** between an animal and its environment — what the
environment offers, given that animal's capabilities. It exists whether or not anyone
perceives it; it is neither an objective property of the object nor a subjective construct;
and it is picked up directly, not inferred from cues.

Norman (1988) used the word to mean the action possibilities a user can **perceive** — in
practice, what an object's appearance suggests. That relocates the affordance into the
object's surface and makes it depend on being noticed. Norman corrected himself in 1999
(real versus perceived affordances) and again in 2013 (introducing "signifiers"), but the
1988 usage is what entered design vocabulary and what students arrive with.

Why it matters here: if affordances are cues, designing them is signposting. If they are
relations between a player's action repertoire and the world's possibilities, then designing
affordances means designing that pairing — which is what Samling 4 is about, what Swink
measures as game feel, and why emergent play works at all.

**Reading order I would suggest**

1. **McGrenere & Ho (2000)** — the clearest side-by-side of what was dropped. Start here.
2. **Norman (1999)**, then the 2013 revision — the corrections in his own words.
3. **Gaver (1991)** — false, hidden and perceptible affordances, solving Norman's problem
   without redefining the term, and eight years earlier.
4. **Chemero (2003)** with **Stoffregen (2003)** — the live disagreement about whether
   affordances are relations or dispositions. **Turvey (1992)** for the dispositional side.
5. **Withagen et al. (2012)** — affordances as invitations. The most designer-facing of these.
6. **Costall (1995)** on canonical affordances; **Heft (2001)** for the deeper history.
7. **Oliver (2005)** and **Torenvliet (2003)** for the case that the term is now unsalvageable
   in design; **Kaptelinin & Nardi (2012)** for an activity-theory way out.
8. **Linderoth (2012)** for the application to gameplay.

**On whether Linderoth is the earliest use in game studies**

Not quite, on what I can verify — though the claim holds in a narrower and more interesting
form.

Linderoth does appear to be the earliest **rigorous, properly Gibsonian** treatment in game
studies: gameplay as a perception–action cycle, exploratory versus performatory acts, and
affordances used to dissolve the digital/non-digital distinction. The DiGRA 2013 paper
"Affordances in games" treats him as the reference point for that position, which suggests
the field saw it that way too.

But the *term* was in game studies well before him, arriving through Norman and HCI rather
than through Gibson. The clearest counter-example I found is **Mateas (2004, and 2001 in
*Digital Creativity*)**, whose "material affordances" and "formal affordances" predate
Linderoth by roughly a decade. Gee (2003) is also worth checking; he uses affordance and
effectivity language, though I could not confirm how Gibsonian it is.

So the defensible version is something like: *the earliest sustained application of Gibson's
ecological psychology to gameplay in game studies* — rather than the earliest use of the
word. I could not settle this definitively; establishing priority properly would mean a
citation search across DiGRA and *Game Studies* from 1998 onward, which is beyond what I can
do reliably here. If it matters to your argument, it is worth asking Linderoth directly.
